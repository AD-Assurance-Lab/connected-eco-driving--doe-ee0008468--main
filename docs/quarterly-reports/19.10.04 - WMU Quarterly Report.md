**Department of Energy Project: Quarterly Report**

**October 4, 2019**

Our team is focused on two primary tasks: (1) developing validated fuel economy and emissions models to that satisfy the upcoming Go/No-Go decision point in January and (2) preparing for the second phase of the project by building capabilities in low error future vehicle velocity prediction as well as fuel efficient control through optimal energy management.

Regarding the first primary task (development of fuel economy and emissions models), our team has identified and developed appropriate models that will be suitable for the project and that will provide the highest possible fidelity. To accurately model emissions our team is using the latest research findings which suggest that artificial neural networks provide improved modeling fidelity when compared to the EPA’s MOVES model which is the current standard. We are building upon this research finding by using novel deep neural networks that further reduce the emissions prediction error. We are using emissions data collected by CSU to develop this emissions model and we can run the MOVES model as a comparison case. We are working with a WMU professor in the computer science department (Alvis Fong) to ensure our deep neural network architecture reflects the latest research findings. For the project’s fuel economy model, our team has determined that FastSim and ADOPT (both developed by NREL) will be a suitable model for the project. We are using the Python version of ADOPT and collaborating with NREL to ensure our vehicle model is exactly what we need for the project. We are validating the model with real world recorded data from CSU. Our group also has a full Autonomie license which we are using for further validation.

Regarding the second primary task (preparing for the second phase of the project), our team has made significant progress preparing velocity prediction algorithms as well as the fuel efficient powertrain control algorithms. Currently we have velocity prediction algorithms that generate low mean absolute error (MAE) values for 10-second horizon prediction. We are using a long short term memory (LSTM) deep neural network, decision trees, and linear regression as comparison cases. We are also exploring the effect of different groups of data inputs on MAE results. We are also working with Alvis Fong in this area and progress has been so significant that we are close to our first publication on the topic. Fuel efficient powertrain control algorithm development has also been a subject of much success. Our team has discovered a technique to significant speed up a dynamic programming optimal control calculation by passing a 3-D matrix of states and controls through the backwards recursion process. We have also developed a 10-second horizon model predictive control algorithm that uses dynamic programming as the optimizer and verified its performance against constant velocity 10-second horizon prediction. We have written a publication on these methods and results and submitted it to the upcoming American Controls Conference. These algorithms will be applied to optimized traffic scenarios in the second phase of the project.
