**Department of Energy Project: Quarterly Report**

**June 26th, 2020**

Our team has completed our two previous tasks: (1) developing validated fuel economy and emissions models to satisfy the previous Go/No-Go decision point and (2) building capabilities in low error future vehicle velocity prediction as well as fuel efficient control through optimal energy management.We have shared our results with the team for Mobility Energy Productivity (MEP) calculations and to begin the discussion on incorporation into Optimal Traffic Management Strategies (Optimal TMS). We are helping to facilitate teamwide biweekly meetings which are critical at this phase now that each of our research components are to be combined. We are currently refining our models, drive cycles, and controls based on initial traffic management results and initial MEP calculation results to identify areas of highest impact as we move towards Optimal Energy Management Strategies (Optimal EMS) and Optimal TMS integration.

Our team has rigorously evaluated numerous high-fidelity modeling software to determine suitable means of modeling fuel economy and emissions from Optimal EMS and Optimal TMS changes in traffic and vehicle operation. We ultimately decided on the following model architecture: Fast-Sim will be used with extra validation from Autonomie to model fuel economy, Autonomie will be used to model CO, and a Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) Deep Neural Network (DNN) will be used to model the emissions. These decisions are based on simulation needs for our project that do not sacrifice fidelity. We have several vehicle models for use in the project and are currently developing an additional model to represent the Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) busses in Fort Collins, CO. The team believes that these might be the key to unlocking the highest MEP.

At this point in the project we have now published one of the world’s most comprehensive studies on velocity prediction using artificial intelligence and deep learning[^1] which directly complements CSU’s recently publish paper on rigorous data collection for advanced automotive applications[^2]. One of the most shocking discoveries was the V2I data is by far the most critical data point for velocity prediction fidelity. It was also discovered that LSTM DNNs provide the best compromise between two error metrics: Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Time Shift.

We are also close to publishing a comprehensive paper on Optimal EMS results. One of the most shocking results from this work was that using constant velocity Model Predictive Control (MPC) with a 10-second horizon resulted in a significant amount of the possible fuel economy improvement. We have also discovered a means to improve the computation time of dynamic programming algorithms that involves using 3-D matrices inside the backwards recursion for loop. Both of these results have been quite shocking to the community. We currently have a journal paper in review that ties the velocity prediction work together with the Optimal EMS work. Efforts are ongoing in this area.

Our fuel economy improvements through velocity prediction and Optimal EMS have been incorporated into an MEP calculation thanks to our project collaborators at NREL. The MEP impact was less than expected so there is currently a big push for all of the team members to stitch each of their research models together to see what can be realized synergistically. CSU and I’s previous published research suggests large fuel economy improvements will be achieved when we add Optimal TMS to our models[^3]. We expect to have Optimal EMS + Optimal TMS results and an associated MEP calculation by the next quarterly report.
